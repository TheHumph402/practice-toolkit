Mental Capacity Assessment

Practical support for completing the MCA element of the Form 3. This page is designed to support assessor reasoning, not provide wording to copy into reports without professional judgement.

← Back to AFA Practice Companion

Purpose of this section

The Mental Capacity Assessment should show how the assessor considered the specific decision, identified the relevant information, took proportionate practicable steps, tested the functional elements, and explained the causal link between impairment and inability.

Step 1 — Identify the specific decision

Prompting questions

  • What is the actual decision being assessed?
  • Is it residence, care, contact, treatment, finances or something else?
  • Is the decision required now or at a reasonably foreseeable future point?
  • Is the decision being framed too broadly?

Common pitfalls

  • Assessing “capacity” generally rather than capacity for a specific decision.
  • Combining several decisions without explaining why.
  • Ignoring material time.

Step 2 — Salient information

Salient information should be decision-specific and proportionate. It should focus on what the person needs to understand in order to make the relevant decision.

Prompts

  • What is the decision?
  • What are the realistic options?
  • What are the reasonably foreseeable consequences?
  • What risks are relevant to this person?
  • What support would be needed if they chose differently?

Common pitfalls

  • Salient information is too broad.
  • Information reads like a care plan rather than decision-specific information.
  • Risks are generic rather than personalised.
  • Too much irrelevant medical or social history is included.

What good looks like

  • Clear and proportionate.
  • Linked to the specific decision.
  • Focused on real options and consequences.
  • Accessible to the person where possible.

Step 3 — Practicable steps

Practicable steps should be reasonable and proportionate. Assessors should not overly pester or pressure a person to engage where they are clearly declining or unable to participate.

Prompts

  • Was the person given information in an accessible way?
  • Was timing considered?
  • Were communication, sensory, language or emotional needs considered?
  • Was collateral evidence used appropriately where the person could not engage?

Common pitfalls

  • Repeated attempts that appear intrusive or coercive.
  • Not explaining why direct testing was limited.
  • Assuming lack of engagement means lack of capacity without wider evidence.

Step 4 — Functional test

Understanding

  • What information was given?
  • What questions were asked?
  • What did the person say or do in response?
  • Could they understand the key information, not just repeat words?

Retention

  • Could the person retain information long enough to use it?
  • Was retention tested proportionately?
  • Was memory impairment relevant to the decision?

Use or weigh

  • Could the person compare options?
  • Could they recognise risk or consequence?
  • Could they apply information to themselves?
  • Were beliefs, delusions, anxiety or executive dysfunction affecting weighing?

Communication

  • Could the person communicate by any means?
  • Were aids, support or alternative communication methods considered?
  • Is communication difficulty separate from inability to decide?

Step 5 — Causal nexus

The assessment should explain how the impairment or disturbance in the functioning of mind or brain causes the inability to make the decision. Diagnosis alone is not enough.

Common pitfall

The report identifies dementia, delirium, psychosis, brain injury or learning disability, but does not explain how that impairment causes the functional inability.

What good looks like

The assessment links the impairment to the specific difficulty with understanding, retaining, using/weighing or communicating in relation to the decision.
